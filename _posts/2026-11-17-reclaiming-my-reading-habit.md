In 2024, I read 40 books. In 2025, I read 24 and I will be grateful if I can finish 20 books by the time 2026 ends. This decline in my reading numbers occurs in parallel to what the internet is calling a 'literacy crisis'. I first came to learn of it from a bookfluencer on Instagram. The irony of that is not lost on me.

The literacy crisis refers to the societal or cultural decline in reading habits or reading comprehension. A study published in the journal *iScience* looked at data from over 236,000 Americans who took part in the American Time Use Survey between 2003 and 2023. It found that fewer people are making time in their day to read for fun. The share of people who read for pleasure on an average day dropped from 28% in 2004 to 16% in 2023, which is roughly one in six. Link to the study [here](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2025.113288).

This has also been the experience of my father and me. Both of us, once voracious and determined readers, now struggle to nurture an attention span that lets us read at length for hours. While the decline in my attention span can be explained by my aggressive consumption of short-form content on social media, it is surprising that my father, with no social media accounts to his name, struggles with the same thing. This anecdotal evidence makes me wonder whether our fragmented attention spans can be attributed to something else. One hypothesis is that even outside of social media, my father is inundated with so much information from texts, emails and TV that it leads to fatigue, and his brain is simply too exhausted to take in more information, even if it's a book he's been looking forward to.

As someone who is very determined not to let my declining attention span get the best of me, I decided to read for 15 minutes every day before going to bed. I have been practising this for the last month and have noticed that I struggle to read a very heavy book, like philosophy or a classic, when I've had a rough week at work. In those weeks, I am also most tempted to scroll endlessly on my phone. I've tried to identify why this happens, and here is what I've found – reading a book is like steering your attention, while scrolling is having your attention pulled. Reading is active. You have to work your brain to focus on a sentence, read it, understand it and move on to the next one. Scrolling works the other way. Every new post grabs your attention for you with bright images, movement, faces and surprises. You don't have to do anything except swipe. Reading delays gratification, while scrolling gives you instant rewards. A tired brain strongly prefers immediate, guaranteed rewards over ones that need patience.

Now, I have adapted my habit to solve for this too. I read my heaviest, most thought-provoking books in the morning on my way to work and reserve the lighter ones for the night. However, more often than not, I am riddled with guilt. Instead of reading indulgent, cheesy fiction, should I not be spending this time on more productive pursuits? Because my free time is so limited, I've found myself viewing it as something to optimise rather than enjoy. Rest feels earned and guilt-free only when it leads to productive outcomes. Wouldn't I much rather use this time learning '10 simple tips to become an AI wizard!'? Productivity culture is deeply ingrained, especially in those of us who were exposed to hyper-competitive environments growing up, and it's something that is still a WIP. (Note to self – your capitalist overlords would love for you to think this way, btw. So as a general rule of life, behave exactly how Big Capitalism does not want you to behave.)

In a similar vein, I have noticed that a lot of first-time readers pick up self-help books because 'at least you get to learn something that way'. In seventh grade, I lent a Dan Brown novel to a non-reader friend of mine, insisting that they would love it. The next day, they returned it to me, saying that they would not be reading it since their mother said 'reading fiction is a waste of time'. I've largely read fiction, aside from the stray biography or two, and I have defended its importance time and again. Fiction helps you exercise your most important muscle – empathy.

No one reads Harry Potter because someday they'll face a Dark Wizard who wants them dead. It isn't a manual for surviving seven years of magic school. It is, however, a lesson in empathy, friendship and bravery. That's what good fiction does. It puts you in someone else's shoes, so you're happy when they're happy and grieve when they grieve. You meet people and realities far removed from your own, and ideas that never come up at your dinner table. Slowly, unfamiliar perspectives stop feeling threatening, and you learn to entertain a notion without accepting it. That's how empathetic children are made, and empathetic children grow into adults who think about how their choices land on other people.

My relationship with reading is like a situationship, perennially oscillating between hot and cold. While I have loved reading since I was in school, in my adult life I have gone from someone who could finish 3 books in a weekend to someone who has been trying to finish the same book for the last 3 months. On this journey, I have discovered a few things that have genuinely helped me rebuild a good reading habit.

## Tips from a recovering reader, if you will

1. For the longest time, I forced myself through books I thought a "serious reader" should be reading, and all it did was make me dread picking them up. If you'd rather read a thriller than a Russian classic, read the thriller. Nobody is handing out awards for suffering through 800 pages you didn't enjoy. **Please, read what you like and NOT what you think you SHOULD be reading.**

2. How to figure out what you like, you ask? Well, to begin with, **READ EVERYTHING.** Fiction, non-fiction, memoirs, poetry, graphic novels, the odd self-help book (no judgement, mostly). You won't know what genuinely pulls you in until you've tried a bit of everything. And if a book isn't working for you 50 pages in, you're allowed to put it down. DNF-ing a book is not a moral failing.

3. **Save your heaviest books for when your brain is fresh, and the lighter ones for when it's fried.** This one I learnt the hard way. Trying to get through philosophy after a rough day at work is a losing battle, and it usually ends with me on my phone. Now the dense stuff goes with me on my morning commute, and the fun, easy reads are for the night.

4. **Carry a book EVERYWHERE.** I have a book in my work bag, one on my bedside table and a few on my Kindle app. I read while waiting for a cab, while having lunch, in that 5-10 minute window before falling asleep. These are the moments we usually reach for our phones without thinking. Having a book (or a Kindle) on you means you have something better to reach for, and those few pages add up faster than you'd expect.

5. And lastly, **set realistic goals and start with reading for 10 mins each day.** Reading is a fun hobby, so treat it as such. Do not set unrealistic standards like 'I will read 50 books this year', and then give up because you could not even read 5. Start slow, make it enjoyable and genuinely have FUN.

As for my 2026 target, I'm not sure I will make it to 20 books, and I am slowly learning to be okay with that. Keeping count of how many books I've read is, after all, just productivity culture in a different outfit, and Big Capitalism would be thrilled if I turned my one indulgence into yet another KPI. So for now, I'm content with my 15 minutes before bed and whichever cheesy novel I'm currently halfway through. My father and I are still very much a WIP when it comes to our attention spans. But somewhere along the way, I stopped asking myself whether my reading is useful and started asking whether I'm enjoying it, and honestly, that has made picking up a book a lot easier.
